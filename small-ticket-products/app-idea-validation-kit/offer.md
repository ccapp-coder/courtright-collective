## The offer page

### Before you spend six months of nights on it.

Every app I have shipped cost me somewhere between twelve and sixteen weeks of evenings. That is real time, taken from a real family, and the worst possible way to spend it is on an idea that was never going to work.

The problem is that you cannot validate your own idea. You are too close, you have already imagined using it, and every friend you describe it to says "oh that's cool" because that is what friends say.

**So I will do the unglamorous part.** Store research, competitor teardowns, and about an hour reading the one and two star reviews of everybody already in your space. Then I write down what I actually think.

**What you get:**

- **A verdict, in the first line.** Build it, build something narrower, or do not build it. No hedging.
- **The market as it stands.** Five competitors minimum, both stores, with ratings, review counts, pricing models, and what each one does badly. Including the honest read on what the shape of that list means, because zero competitors usually means no demand rather than open field.
- **What people are actually complaining about.** The most valuable section in the document. I read the one and two star reviews of your competitors, group the complaints, count them, and quote them verbatim. Those quotes are also your marketing copy, written by your customers, better than either of us would write it.
- **Where your people are.** The communities, groups, and forums where your audience already talks, with sizes, and an honest answer on whether you can reach them without paying for ads.
- **A scoping score,** out of 30, against the six questions I use on my own projects. Including the one nobody asks: is this useful with zero other users?
- **The smallest version worth building,** with what goes in v1, what you fake by hand at first, and what waits.
- **The money arithmetic.** What it looks like at 100, 500, and 1,000 paying users, and what it realistically takes to get there in your category. Most people have never actually worked this out.
- **A two-week test** you run before writing a line of code, with kill criteria written as numbers in advance so you cannot quietly move them later.
- **The build order,** if the answer is yes.

**$149. Five business days. Plus a 20-minute call to argue with me about any of it.**

> Here is the part that matters: **I am willing to tell you no.** Roughly one in three of these comes back as a no or a build-something-narrower, and if it did not, the whole thing would be worthless. Killing an idea in week two is the cheapest possible outcome for a bad idea, and it is worth a lot more than $149.

## Notes and assumptions

- **Priced at $149.** It is 3 to 4 hours of real research, so the rate is honest. It also sits at the price where somebody serious about their idea will pay it and somebody idly daydreaming will not, which is exactly the filter you want.
- **Time budget is 3 to 4 hours,** broken down in the internal notes. The review-reading hour is the one that must not be rushed, because it is what people are actually buying even though they do not know it yet.
- **The willingness to say no is the product.** A validation service that always says build it becomes worthless the moment two customers compare notes. The internal notes set the expectation explicitly: about one in three should be a no.
- Never promises download numbers, revenue, or store performance. Ranges and reasoning only. That is both honest and the right posture legally.
- The revenue arithmetic section is the one most likely to change somebody's mind, because seeing "1,000 paying subscribers" written next to what that actually requires is clarifying in a way no amount of advice is.
- **Ambiguity call, noted as instructed.** The brief called this a "kit," which could mean a self-serve DIY product. Built as a service, because the value is in somebody else doing the research the founder cannot do objectively. The DIY version of this already exists in the library as Phase 2 of the Launch Playbook, which makes the two products complementary rather than competing.
- Best upsell path: Validation, then Application Creation or the partnership program. A no still often converts later, on a different idea.
