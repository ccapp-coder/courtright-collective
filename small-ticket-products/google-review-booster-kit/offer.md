## Sales blurb

**Your happy customers are not withholding reviews. Nobody asked them.**

That is the entire problem. They walked out the door thinking about lunch and the thought never occurred to them. The gap between "would happily review you" and "actually reviewed you" is almost entirely a gap of asking, at the right moment, with a link that takes one tap.

**Inside the kit:**

**The guide.** How to get your one-tap review link (both methods, because Google keeps moving the button), how to shorten it to `yoursite.com/review`, and where to put the QR code so it lands where customers' hands already are.

**The rules, stated plainly,** because these are the ones that get profiles restricted. Never offer anything in exchange, not even for "an honest review." Never review-gate, which means asking how somebody feels first and only sending the link to the ones who say something nice. That one is explicitly against Google's policy and it is the rule small businesses break most often, usually without knowing it.

**The timing,** which matters more than the wording. The best moment to ask is immediately after a customer says something good. Not later that week. Right then. The guide lists the exact seven moments to watch for.

**40+ templates.** In-person asks written word for word, for restaurants, service businesses, retail, repeat customers, and referrals. Text messages under 160 characters. Emails, long and short, plus the P.S. that goes on every invoice. Printed counter cards, table tents, and handoff cards.

**Every reply you will ever need to write.** Five star, four star, mixed, and six different flavors of one star: the fair complaint, the service failure, the wait time, the unfair one, the wrong business, and the one that is clearly a competitor. Each on a four-sentence structure that takes responsibility without arguing.

**Two trackers.** One logs asks, which is the number you actually control. One logs the monthly scoreboard: ask rate, conversion rate, total reviews, average rating, and replies. The sample data tells its own story, from a month where nobody asked to a month where asking became normal.

**$39. Set up in 30 minutes. Then it is a habit, not a project.**

> A realistic expectation, because nobody else will give you one: if you ask every customer and the link is one tap, expect 5 to 15 percent to actually leave a review. Thirty asks a month is a few reviews a month, which is about forty a year, which puts you ahead of nearly every competitor on your street. Slow and steady also looks natural. Twenty reviews in one afternoon looks purchased, and Google's filters agree.

## Notes and assumptions

- Priced at $39. Review management apps run $50 to $200 a month, so a one-time $39 that does the same job by hand is an easy comparison to make on the sales page.
- **Compliance is a feature here, and it is stated correctly.** No incentives, no review gating, no self-reviews, no kiosk clustering. Getting this wrong in a product would genuinely hurt a buyer's business, so the guide says it plainly and the templates are all built to follow it, including an ask script for "when you are not sure how they felt" that asks everyone the same way.
- Deliberately avoids naming specific Google Business Profile menu paths as if they were permanent, because Google moves them constantly. It gives the current path and the Place ID fallback method, which does not change.
- The tracker's real insight is **counting asks, not reviews.** That reframes the problem from something the buyer cannot control to something they can, and it is the thing that makes the kit work.
- Cross-sells the free QR Menu Maker for generating the review QR code, which is the library funneling into itself.
- Best upsell path: Kit, then Get-Found-on-Google Mini-Audit, then Email Automation to send the requests automatically.
- Good bundle partner: the Mini-Audit service. Call it "The Local Visibility Bundle" at $99.
